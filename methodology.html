1. Scope and principles

ReachGrid supports planning and screening. It does not certify electrical capacity, land rights, permits, safety, charger uptime or profitability. It optimises the marginal value of each proposed site, not the number of chargers on a map. Every value carries a provenance tag: observed from a source, derived by the pipeline, or imputed where a source had to be reallocated. Missing data is never treated as zero, and a mapped charger is never assumed to be operational.

2. Data sources and governance

The pipeline (pipeline/build.py) is deterministic. It records a SHA-256 checksum for every input and stamps each build YYYY.MM.DD-hash. A scheduled workflow re-fetches all sources weekly, rebuilds, runs the acceptance tests and publishes only builds whose data changed, with a per-state change report.

3. Demand model

Census crosswalk. Census 2011 publishes 640 districts; today there are 737. Each 2011 district polygon (DataMeet) is intersected with current boundaries. Its totals (population, households, vehicle ownership, income bands) are split across the overlapping current districts by a mix of overlapping area and overlapping GeoNames town population:

share(j) = (1 − a)·area(c ∩ j)/area(c) + a·towns(c ∩ j)/towns(c), a = min(0.8, town population / census population)

A district is observed when one 2011 district supplies ≥ 90% of its population and gives ≥ 90% of its own; otherwise imputed. National totals reconcile to the Census within 0.01%.

Demand surface. Each district is tiled with about 260 hexagonal cells (0.8–9 km across). The urban population share (urban households ÷ households) is placed around GeoNames towns with a Gaussian kernel, σ = max(0.9·cell size, 1.4·√(pop/50,000)) km, capped at 15 km. The rural share is spread evenly. Cell weight wᵢ = estimated people.

EV pressure. Vehicle-owning households ≈ households with a car + 0.25 × households with a two-wheeler (Census 2011). If the user uploads VAHAN EV registrations by district, those replace the proxy for pressure and the commercial screen.

4. Reachability

Aᵢ = 1[ min over chargers s of d(i, s) ≤ τ ] weighted coverage = Σ wᵢAᵢ / Σ wᵢ

Distance metrics. (a) Straight line × detour factor (default 1.3, user-set), a labelled proxy. (b) Highway-network routing: a multi-source Dijkstra from every charger over the 14,414-node Natural Earth highway graph, with access legs of up to 12 km at the detour factor and direct straight-line access under 4 km. The national charger set is always used, so chargers across a district or state border count. τ is an analytical setting (default 10 km), not a regulatory standard; the Compare tab tests other values.

5. Candidate sites

  • Towns (GeoNames, ≥ 2,000 people) with no listed charger within 1 km.
  • Highway sites every 8 km along major roads, more than 3 km from any charger.
  • Demand hotspots: the most populous cells beyond 0.6·τ.
  • Evidence sites uploaded by the user.

Sites within 1.5 km of each other are merged. A state-wide run keeps up to 2,500, interleaving the three types so that none crowds out the others.

6. Scoring

Priority(c) = 0.30·Gap + 0.25·EVPressure + 0.20·Corridor + 0.15·Equity + 0.10·Feasibility
  • Gap: people within τ of c who are currently beyond τ.
  • EV pressure: vehicle households in reach ÷ (1 + chargers in reach).
  • Corridor: max(0, 1 − distance to a major highway / 10 km).
  • Equity: unserved people weighted by how far they are now, capped at 3τ.
  • Feasibility: from host type, or from uploaded field evidence (spare kVA, land status). Unknown for hotspots.

Gap, pressure and equity are scaled to 0–100 within the candidate set. If a component is unavailable it is dropped and the remaining weights are renormalised; it is never set to zero. Weights are design hypotheses, adjustable in the workspace and stress-tested in the sensitivity analysis.

7. Optimisation

maximise Σ wᵢ·1[ min d(i, S ∪ X) ≤ τ ] subject to Σ cⱼxⱼ ≤ B, xⱼ ∈ {0,1}

Greedy marginal gain (Appendix A): repeatedly add the feasible site with the largest newly-covered weight per rupee, recomputing coverage after each pick. Local search then tries 1-swaps and additions that raise coverage within budget. Exact integer programme: the budgeted maximum-coverage ILP is solved by LP-based branch and bound (jsLPSolver) in a background worker. Its LP relaxation gives an upper bound, so the report states how close greedy gets (e.g. greedy reaches 93–97% of the bound in tested districts). Baselines: score-only ranking and an EV/charger-ratio ranking. A budget curve shows the diminishing returns across eight budget levels.

8. Planning objectives

  • Coverage: greedy maximum coverage per rupee.
  • Equity: coverage gain weighted by (1 + baseline distance/τ, capped at 3), favouring the worst served.
  • Corridor reach: the same objective restricted to highway sites. A separate corridor check routes between any two towns, finds charging gaps along the route and tests them against an EV's usable range.
  • Commercial screening: maximises vehicle demand captured per rupee, sharing each cell's demand equally among all chargers that reach it. It reports sessions/day, energy, net margin and payback under editable assumptions (EV share, sessions per EV, kWh per session, ₹ margin per kWh, opex), or under a factor recalibrated from observed portfolio usage.

9. Confidence

Confidence is a transparent data-quality rubric shown separately from the score: census provenance, cross-verification of nearby listings (share in ≥ 2 sources), the distance metric used, and host feasibility evidence. It is a quality indicator, not a probability. A high score with low confidence is a validation priority, not a construction recommendation.

10. Validation

Automated acceptance tests (proposal §8) run on every deploy and in the browser (Validate → self-tests):

  • Coordinates are valid and the census reconciles to the national total.
  • Adding a charger never increases any distance.
  • Coverage is monotone in τ.
  • Every plan stays within budget, and blocked sites are never chosen.
  • Unknown components are renormalised.
  • Identical inputs give identical outputs.
  • ILP ≤ LP bound, and local search ≥ greedy.
  • Runtime stays within limits (the largest state, Uttar Pradesh, runs in under 0.5 s).

Holdout backtest. Hide 20% of a region's real chargers, re-plan without them, then measure the hit rate (proposed sites within 3 km of a hidden one), recall, and the coverage restored compared with the real hidden sites. Findings in tested regions:

  • The census demand proxy alone lands on real operator locations 1.3–3× more often than random (Pune 22% vs 13%, Nashik 18% vs 6%, Bengaluru Urban 77% vs 59%, Jharkhand 9% vs 5%). This supports the demand proxy, the main risk named in §12.
  • The coverage plan rarely coincides with operator sites, by design. It restores more coverage than the operators' real sites did (Jharkhand 51% vs 42%; Pune 100% vs 87%).

Sensitivity. Nine variants of τ, detour, ±30% costs and alternative weights, reported as shortlist overlap (Jaccard similarity) and coverage, exportable as CSV or JSON.

11. Portfolio monitoring and recalibration

Sites marked as built become existing chargers in every later analysis. When observed sessions/day are recorded, ReachGrid fits observed ≈ k × predicted vehicle households captured (least squares through the origin) and reports k, R² and mean absolute percentage error. The commercial screen can then use the calibrated k instead of assumed adoption rates.

12. Limitations

  • Charger listings are point-in-time: power and tariff are missing for about 45%, and operational status is not observed.
  • Demand is Census 2011. Growth since then is not modelled unless EV registrations are uploaded.
  • The highway graph is 1:10m: good for screening and corridors, not navigation.
  • Grid capacity, land, permits and safety are unknown unless the user supplies evidence.
  • Cost and commercial figures are hypotheses for discussion, not quotes or forecasts.
  • Roles, review and the audit log run client-side. They enforce workflow discipline in the browser; a multi-user deployment would enforce them on a server.
